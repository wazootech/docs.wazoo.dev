Human progress tracks how information is stored, shared, and processed. In every
era, a new medium has changed what a society could record and pass on. Ochre
paintings, clay tablets, print, and spreadsheets each extended that record.

For most of human history, the brain was the only hard drive. Early societies
used [myth and song](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oral_tradition) to encode
critical data across generations. The Agricultural Revolution produced
[Cuneiform](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cuneiform), and the
[Phoenician alphabet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Phoenician_alphabet)
democratized literacy. The
[printing press](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Printing_press) collapsed the
cost of replication and fueled the
[Renaissance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Renaissance).

In 1945, the [Memex](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Memex) described an
information system that linked facts together, a design that anticipated the
modern [knowledge graph](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Knowledge_graph).
[Hypertext](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hypertext) applied that representation
to digital information systems, and graphical environments such as
[Xerox PARC](<https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/PARC_(company)>) and Apple's
[HyperCard](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/HyperCard) refined it. In 1999, the
[W3C](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/World_Wide_Web_Consortium) standardized
[RDF](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Resource_Description_Framework), which
defined the interoperable primitives of linked data.

With the rise of autonomous agents, the externalized hard drive can now read,
synthesize, pattern-match, and generate artifacts. But modern AI systems are
stateless out of the box: advanced reasoning engines with no memory of past
interactions.

Worlds is the engine for personalized software. It gives autonomous agents the
stateful context they need to move from statistical mimicry toward intentional
agency. It fuses Large Language Models with the Semantic Web, using a
[knowledge graph](/projects/worlds) that steers behavior through individual
preference data.

We believe computing still falls short of its potential for people. Software is
about to eat the world again.
